How this works, in plain words

Conversion

Of the deals that left a stage, the share that moved forward. A deal that skipped a stage counts as passing through it. Deals still sitting in the stage wait until they move, so a young pipeline does not drag the rate down.

Time in stage and the stall line

Days from entering a stage to entering the next one, or to closing. The stall line is the day by which three in four deals that moved on had already left. A deal past it is behind its own pipeline's pace.

Where lost deals die

Each lost deal is counted once, at the last stage it reached. Count shows where the volume leaks. Value shows where the money leaks. Early deals often have no amount, so early lost value reads low.

The fix worth the most

For each stage: last year's deal volume, times the gap between today's rate and the best quarter, times the share of deals that win after moving on, times the average won deal. The target is a rate this team already hit, not an industry benchmark.

Holds up or could be luck

Pick the best of ten quarters and one will look good by chance. The skill tests the best quarter against all the others. Holds up means the gap is too big to be a lucky draw. Could be luck means it is not. Find out what was different in that quarter before you spend on it.

When not to trust it

Under 20 deals behind a rate, you see counts, not percentages. Stage dates entered in bulk after the fact make time in stage meaningless. Slow deals lose more, and a slow deal is often a weak deal: speed is a warning sign, not proof that rushing wins. A best quarter can come from a different market, a different rep or a different deal mix. Find the cause before you copy it.
